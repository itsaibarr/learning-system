# Learning science for the engine: unit size, sequence, questioning, pacing

Researched 2026-10-07. Problem: one chapter became 29 concepts and 101 questions; two school lessons of content took 2+ hours and did not finish. Short answer from the evidence: teach whole schemas, not fragments; question to make decisions, not to confirm each fact; space the retrieval across days instead of massing it in one session.

## Chunking and unit size

- **Complexity is relative to the learner.** Element interactivity is how many elements must be held in mind at once to understand something; it "depends on a combination of both the nature of the information and the knowledge of the person processing" it (7). The same material is many elements for a novice and one element for an expert.
- **Group what interacts.** If ideas only make sense together (a law, its conditions, and its consequence), the understanding *is* the interaction. Splitting them into separately-questioned "concepts" tests fragments and never the schema.
- **Split only for a true novice.** The isolated-interacting elements effect: for very high interactivity material, teach elements in isolation first, then the full version. Pollock et al. found this helped low-knowledge learners only, with no difference for learners with more prior knowledge (8).
- **Isolated facts need no teaching block.** For low-interactivity material (definitions), a *reverse* worked-example effect appears: generating the answer beats being shown it (7).
- **Expertise reversal.** Guidance that helps novices "first decrease[s] in size, then disappear[s], and can eventually reverse" as expertise grows; worked examples should be "faded out and replaced by problems" (7).
- **Unit length.** The best maths teachers spent about 23 of 40 minutes presenting, demonstrating, questioning and working examples (least effective: 11), as "a series of short presentations" each followed by practice (1). "Small steps" are small relative to working memory, so they grow with prior knowledge (7, inference). Bloom's mastery units ended with a formative test after about two weeks of lessons (11). 4C/ID orders whole tasks simple to complex and fades support within each level (7).

## Sequencing

Rosenshine (1) as a tutoring loop:

1. **Daily review**, 5-8 minutes: due items and the prerequisites today's lesson needs.
2. **Present one small step** with a model or worked example; practise right after.
3. **Guided practice** through questions, including process questions ("explain how you got it").
4. **Check understanding** by having the learner summarise, explain or apply. Never "any questions?".
5. **High success rate**: best classrooms 82% correct versus 73% for the weakest; "the optimal success rate ... appears to be about 80 percent".
6. **Scaffolds**, withdrawn as competence grows.
7. **Independent practice** on the same material or a slight variation.
8. **Weekly and monthly review**: last week's work every Monday, last month's every fourth Monday.

Within a step: worked example, then completion problems with less and less given, then full problems (the completion strategy, 7). Theory ("supportive information") comes before tasks; how-to tips come just in time (7). Interleaving has moderate utility: practise a new problem type alone first, then mix it with earlier types the learner must tell apart. It fails when students lack practice on each type (2).

## How much to question

- **Retrieval works, but massing it does not.** Practice testing is rated high utility. More correct recalls help "with diminishing returns" (4-5 versus 1 recall: better). But repeated tests "in immediate succession" give "minimal or nonexistent effects"; spaced repeats give sizable ones (2). Four questions in a row on one concept is mostly massed overhead.
- **Generative beats recognition**: recall and short answer beat fill-in-the-blank or recognition (2). Testing with feedback is "extremely robust" (2).
- **About 80% correct is the difficulty signal** (1). An item answered right 98% of the time teaches little and tells nothing (inference).
- **Questions must reveal thinking.** Rapid factual questioning becomes "a ritual" (3). Use hinge questions where each wrong option maps to a misconception (5), and Rosenshine's relational stems: "How are X and Y alike?", "How does X tie in with what we learned before?" (1). Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation have moderate utility (2).
- **Finer is not better.** Step-level tutoring (d = 0.76) nearly matched human tutoring (d = 0.79) (12); secondary sources report substep-level systems were no better. Per-fact questioning is the substep level.
- Practice is formative only if its evidence changes "the next steps" (4). A question whose every answer leads to the same next action is overhead (inference).

## Moving on and revisiting

- **Mastery criterion.** Bloom: formative test, corrective work, parallel test, mastery standard 80% (11). Rosenshine: master each point before the next; reteach when necessary; stop practice if errors are being practised (1).
- **Successive relearning.** Practise retrieval until each item is correct once in a session (missed items go to the back of the stack), then relearn in later sessions (10). Rawson & Dunlosky (2011) recommend "an initial criterion of 3 correct recalls and then to relearn them 3 times at widely spaced intervals"; initial criterion matters less once relearning happens (9). In a real course: over 10% better exam scores, and over 60% versus under 20% of concepts recalled 24 days later (10). Relearning is fast: 30-40 minutes to learn 8-10 lengthy definitions, about 15 minutes for the first relearning, under 5 for the next (10). For problem solving the gain was smaller (d = .28) (10).
- **Spacing lag** best at 10-20% of how long the knowledge must last (Cepeda et al. 2008, via 2).
- **Misconception versus slip.** Misconceptions form when material is new and background weak, and learned errors "are very difficult to overcome" (1). Feedback fixes high-confidence errors especially well, and practice errors do little harm when feedback follows (2). Widespread misconception: reteach; individual difficulty: scaffolded feedback, a hint rather than the answer (5).

## Adaptation

- **Diagnose fast, then tailor.** Kalyuga's tutors set the starting guidance level from a rapid pretest. Learners asked for a first step or to verify solution steps; this correlated up to .92 with full tests at up to 4.9 times less time. Adapted instruction beat non-adapted (effect sizes 0.46; 0.55 and 0.69 with an efficiency measure) (8).
- **Concrete advance rule (8):** move to the next, harder class of tasks when the learner verifies all steps correctly *and* rates difficulty below 5 on a 9-point scale.
- **Fading pace (8):** knowledgeable learners did better when one worked step was removed per example (or problems straight away); novices did better at one step per two examples.
- **Challenge** must be accepted and must not overload; do not ask for new cognitive and metacognitive skills at once (6).
- **Feedback** on the task, subject or self-regulation, not the person; timing evidence is inconclusive, as immediate, during-task and up-to-a-week feedback had similar effects (5).

## Decision rules for the engine

1. **Unit = one schema.** A unit holds 2-4 steps and fits one 40-minute lesson. A chapter worth two lessons is 2 units and at most about 8 steps, never 29 concepts. (inference from 1, 7)
2. **Merge interacting ideas into one step.** Split into isolated-then-combined only if the diagnostic shows the learner is a novice here. (7, 8)
3. **No teaching block for an isolated fact.** State it inside the step it serves and test it by recall in review. (7; inference)
4. **Open every session with a 5-8 minute review** of due items and today's prerequisites. (1)
5. **Diagnose before teaching.** Ask 2-3 rapid first-step or verification items per unit. All right with low effort: skip worked examples and go straight to problems. Otherwise: worked example first. (8)
6. **Step pattern:** short explanation, worked example, completion problem, full problem. Fade one worked step per example if the learner is succeeding, one per two examples if not. (7, 8)
7. **Question budget:** one diagnostic question per step, one or two relational or synthesis questions per unit, and one transfer item. About 8-12 graded questions per 40-minute unit. Never two consecutive questions on the same item. (2, 1; numbers are inference)
8. **Prefer generative questions** (explain, predict, compare, apply). Every multiple-choice distractor must map to a named misconception. (2, 5)
9. **Difficulty band:** track success over the last 10 graded answers. 70-90%: hold. Above 90%: skip remaining guided practice, fade support, raise complexity. Below 70%: smaller step, add a worked example or reteach. (1 for 80%; band edges are inference)
10. **Advance within a session** when the learner answers one unseen application question correctly and explains why. If difficulty ratings are collected, also require "easy" (below 5 of 9). No per-fact streaks. (8; inference)
11. **Durable = successive relearning:** retrieved correctly in 3 later sessions, each to one correct recall, on separate days. Keep relearning sessions short. (9, 10)
12. **Spacing:** next review at 10-20% of the time until the knowledge is needed. Keep the 1/3/7/21-day boxes, but compress them to fit an exam date. (2)
13. **Slip vs misconception:** a slip is right on immediate retry or a minor recurrent error inside correct reasoning, so log it and move on. A misconception is the same wrong model on two differently-worded items, or a confident wrong explanation: contrast case, hinge question, recheck next session. (1, 2, 5; criteria are inference)
14. **Feedback:** say what is wrong and what to do next. Give a hint before the answer. Never praise the person. Correct immediately only errors the learner cannot detect alone. (5, 3)
15. **Interleave and review cumulatively.** Mix problem types only after each has been practised alone once. Every weekly review mixes earlier units; every fourth week, review the month. (2, 1)
